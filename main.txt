\textbf{\large ABSTRACT}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.4cm}

\noindent
We present a comprehensive statistical analysis of exoplanet habitability
using the \href{https://exoplanetarchive.ipac.caltech.edu}{NASA Exoplanet Archive}
dataset as of 1 May 2026, comprising \textbf{6,278 confirmed planets}.
Our study combines six major data products, including PSCompPars,
PS ($N = 39,816$ solutions), TD, STELLARHOSTS, microlensing
($N = 883$), and direct imaging ($N = 49$).

We define potentially habitable planet candidates (PHCs) using three
constraints: habitable-zone insolation ($0.2 \leq S/\Searth \leq 1.8$),
Earth-sized radii ($0.5 \leq R_p/\Rearth \leq 1.5$), and equilibrium
temperatures ($200 \leq \Teq \leq 370\,\mathrm{K}$). This yields
\textbf{29 PHCs}, of which \textbf{22 orbit M-dwarf stars}. The nearest
candidate is Proxima Centauri~b ($1.30\,\mathrm{pc}$), while TRAPPIST-1
hosts the largest concentration (four PHCs).

We investigate key population trends, including the Fulton radius gap,
the stellar metallicity--giant-planet correlation, and wide-orbit planet
demographics from microlensing and direct imaging. A 25-figure atlas is
constructed to visualise the multi-dimensional structure of the
exoplanet population.

The current census is strongly biased toward short-period planets due to
transit selection effects, leading to an under-sampled habitable zone
around Sun-like stars. Correcting for these biases suggests that the
Milky Way may host $\sim 10^{10}$--$10^{11}$ potentially habitable rocky
planets. Future facilities, including \textit{PLATO}, the Habitable Worlds
Observatory, and the Extremely Large Telescopes, will be essential for
detecting and characterising true Earth analogues.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The search for habitable worlds beyond the Solar System is a central goal of
modern astrophysics. Since the first detection of an exoplanet around a
Sun-like star, 51~Pegasi~b, by \citet{MayorQueloz1995}, the field has expanded
rapidly. What began with a small number of gas-giant detections has evolved
into a population of over 6,000 confirmed exoplanets, spanning a wide range
of masses, radii, orbital architectures, and stellar environments.

A major step forward came with the \textit{Kepler} mission
\citep{Borucki2010}, which provided the first statistically meaningful
measurement of planetary occurrence rates. Its results established that
small planets ($R_p < 4\,\Rearth$) are common in the Galaxy
\citep{Fressin2013, Petigura2013}. The Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite
(\textit{TESS}; \citealt{Ricker2015}) has since extended this work to nearby
bright stars, enabling detailed follow-up studies, particularly with the
James Webb Space Telescope (\textit{JWST}; \citealt{Gardner2023}).

Habitability is commonly defined as the ability of a planet to sustain liquid
water on its surface \citep{Kasting1993}. This motivates the concept of the
circumstellar habitable zone (HZ), the range of orbital distances within
which a rocky planet with a CO$_2$--H$_2$O--N$_2$ atmosphere can maintain
surface liquid water \citep{Kopparapu2013, Kopparapu2014}. However, the HZ
alone does not fully determine habitability. Additional factors such as
planetary mass and composition, orbital eccentricity, tidal locking,
stellar activity, and long-term climate regulation are also important
\citep{Shields2016}.

In this work, we present a comprehensive, data-driven analysis of exoplanet
habitability using the 1 May 2026 release of the NASA Exoplanet Archive
\citep{NEA2026}. We combine six independent data products spanning all major
detection methods and construct a 25-figure atlas to explore key trends in
planetary and stellar properties. This approach enables a multi-dimensional
view of the current habitable-planet census and provides constraints on the
distribution of potentially habitable worlds in the Galaxy.

The paper is organised as follows. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data
and methodology. Sections~\ref{sec:discovery}--\ref{sec:di} present the main
results. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the astrophysical implications,
and Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} summarises our conclusions.


\section{Data and Methods}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{NASA Exoplanet Archive Data Products}

All data were retrieved on 25 April 2026 from the NASA Exoplanet Archive
(\url{https://exoplanetarchive.ipac.caltech.edu}). We use six primary
data tables:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{PSCompPars}: The Planetary Systems Composite Parameters
  table provides one entry per confirmed planet, containing best-estimate
  physical and orbital properties compiled from multiple published
  solutions ($N = 6{,}278$).

  \item \textbf{PS}: The Planetary Systems table includes all available
  parameter solutions for each planet, allowing direct comparison between
  independent analyses ($N = 39{,}816$).

  \item \textbf{TD}: The Transit Detections table lists parameters derived
  from individual transit fits, providing detailed constraints on transit
  geometry and timing ($N = 36{,}068$).

  \item \textbf{STELLARHOSTS}: This catalogue contains host-star properties,
  including spectral type, effective temperature ($\Teff$), stellar mass
  ($M_\star$), radius ($R_\star$), metallicity ($\FeH$), and surface gravity
  ($\logg$) ($N = 47{,}558$).

  \item \textbf{ML}: The Microlensing dataset includes physical parameters
  of planets detected via gravitational microlensing, primarily probing
  wide-orbit systems ($N = 883$).

  \item \textbf{DI}: The Direct Imaging catalogue provides measurements of
  angular separations, near-infrared contrast ratios, and orbital constraints
  for directly imaged planets ($N = 49$).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Habitability Criteria}
\label{sec:criteria}

We define a \emph{potentially habitable candidate} (PHC) as a planet that
simultaneously satisfies:
\begin{align}
  0.2\,\Searth &\leq S_p \leq 1.8\,\Searth,  \label{eq:insol} \\
  0.5\,\Rearth &\leq R_p \leq 1.5\,\Rearth.  \label{eq:radius}
\end{align}

The insolation limits in Equation~(\ref{eq:insol}) follow the conservative
habitable-zone prescription of \citet{Kopparapu2013}, bounded by the
moist-greenhouse inner edge and the maximum-greenhouse outer edge.
Equation~(\ref{eq:radius}) restricts the sample to predominantly rocky
planets. The upper bound is motivated by the observed Fulton gap at
$\sim 1.5$--$2.0\,\Rearth$ \citep{Fulton2017}, which marks the transition
between rocky and volatile-rich planets, while the lower bound excludes
objects unlikely to retain substantial atmospheres.

The planetary equilibrium temperature is estimated as:
\begin{equation}
  \Teq = T_{\star}
  \left(\frac{R_\star}{2a}\right)^{1/2}
  \left(1 - A_B\right)^{1/4},
  \label{eq:teq}
\end{equation}
where $A_B$ is the Bond albedo (assumed to be 0.30), $T_\star$ is the
stellar effective temperature, $R_\star$ is the stellar radius, and $a$
is the orbital semi-major axis.

\subsection{Habitable Zone Boundaries}
\label{sec:hzbounds}

We adopt the analytic formulation of the habitable zone from
\citet{Kopparapu2013}, expressed as:
\begin{equation}
  S_\mathrm{eff}(\Teff) =
  S_{\mathrm{eff},\odot}
  + a_1 T_\star + a_2 T_\star^2
  + a_3 T_\star^3 + a_4 T_\star^4,
  \label{eq:kopparapu}
\end{equation}
where $T_\star = \Teff - 5780\,\mathrm{K}$ and the coefficients
$\{a_i\}$ depend on the specific habitable-zone boundary. We consider both
conservative (moist greenhouse, maximum greenhouse) and optimistic
(runaway greenhouse, early Mars) limits, and overlay the resulting
$S_\mathrm{eff}$--$\Teff$ relations on the observed planet population
(Figure~\ref{fig:kopparapu}).

\subsection{Statistical Treatment}

All analysis was performed in Python using widely used scientific
computing libraries, including \texttt{pandas}, \texttt{NumPy},
\texttt{SciPy}, and \texttt{Matplotlib}, for data handling,
numerical computation, and visualisation.

Given the strongly non-Gaussian nature of most planetary-parameter
distributions, we characterise them using the median and median absolute
deviation (MAD) rather than the mean and standard deviation. Measurements
flagged as limits (\texttt{pl\_lim $\neq 0$}) are excluded from all fitting
and binning procedures.

\subsection{Discovery Methods and Temporal Trends}

Figure~\ref{fig:discovery} summarises the distribution of confirmed
exoplanets by detection method, along with the temporal evolution of
discovery rates since 1992.

\begin{figure}[t!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figs/fig01_discovery_overview.png}
  \caption{
    \textit{Left:} Distribution of 6,278 confirmed exoplanets by discovery
    method. Transit photometry dominates (73.9\%), reflecting the sensitivity
    of \textit{Kepler} and \textit{TESS} to short-period systems.
    \textit{Right:} Annual discovery rate. Dashed vertical lines indicate
    the launch of \textit{Kepler} (2009) and \textit{TESS} (2018).
    The pronounced peak in 2016 ($N = 1,284$) corresponds to a major
    \textit{Kepler} data-validation release.
  }
  \label{fig:discovery}
\end{figure}

Transit photometry accounts for 4,640 confirmed planets (73.9\%),
and therefore dominates the current census. This reflects both the
geometric transit probability ($P_\mathrm{tr} \propto R_\star/a$) and
the exceptional statistical power of \textit{Kepler}'s continuous,
high-cadence observations. The radial-velocity (RV) method contributes
1,180 planets (18.8\%), providing direct mass measurements that are
essential for interpreting transit-derived radii and bulk densities.

Gravitational microlensing contributes 278 planets (4.4\%), uniquely
probing wide-orbit ($\sim 1$-$10\,\mathrm{AU}$) systems at kiloparsec
distances, a regime largely inaccessible to transit surveys. Direct
imaging accounts for 97 planets (1.5\%), limited by extreme flux-contrast
requirements, but offering the only direct access to young, luminous
giant planets on wide orbits ($>5\,\mathrm{AU}$).

Figure~\ref{fig:cumulative} presents the cumulative discovery rate by
method on a logarithmic scale, illustrating their distinct growth
trajectories.

\begin{figure}[t!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figs/fig02_cumulative_discovery.png}
  \caption{
    Cumulative confirmed exoplanet count by detection method from 1992 to 2026
    (logarithmic $y$-axis). Vertical dashed lines mark the launch of
    \textit{Kepler} (2009) and \textit{TESS} (2018), while the dotted line
    indicates \textit{JWST} (2022). Transit detections exhibit rapid,
    near-exponential growth after 2009, whereas RV detections increase
    more steadily. Microlensing shows episodic growth driven by survey
    campaigns (OGLE, MOA, KMTNet).
  }
  \label{fig:cumulative}
\end{figure}

Prior to 2009, the confirmed exoplanet sample comprised only
$\sim 400$ planets, dominated by RV-detected hot Jupiters.
The \textit{Kepler} mission fundamentally reshaped this landscape,
delivering more than 2,600 planets and establishing that small,
rocky planets are the most common outcome of planet formation
\citep{Fressin2013}. 

Following this, \textit{TESS} has sustained a discovery rate of
$\sim 200$-$400\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$, with an emphasis on nearby
($<200$~pc) bright host stars. This strategy provides a critical
bridge between statistical surveys and detailed atmospheric
characterisation, particularly with facilities such as
\textit{JWST}.


\subsection{Habitable Zone Analysis}

Out of 6,278 confirmed exoplanets, 139 satisfy the conservative
insolation criterion ($0.2 \leq S_p/\Searth \leq 1.8$) and are
therefore located within the habitable zone (HZ). Independently,
999 planets fall within the Earth-sized radius range
($0.5$--$1.5\,\Rearth$). The intersection of these two criteria
yields a sample of \textbf{29 potentially habitable candidates (PHCs)}.

Figure~\ref{fig:mass_radius} places this selection in the broader
context of the exoplanet population by showing the mass - radius
distribution on a logarithmic scale.

\begin{figure}[t!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figs/fig03_mass_radius.png}
  \caption{
    Mass- radius diagram of confirmed exoplanets (log - log scale),
    colour coded by detection method. The green shaded region indicates
    the Earth-like radius regime ($\sim 0.5$ - $1.5\,\Rearth$), while the red band marks the Fulton radius gap ($\sim 1.5$ - $2.0\,\Rearth$).
    Vertical dotted lines denote characteristic mass scales:
    $1\,M_{\oplus}$, $1\,M_{\mathrm{Jup}}$, and the brown dwarf limit
    ($13\,M_{\mathrm{Jup}}$). The overlaid curve illustrates the
    theoretical mass - radius relation, highlighting the transition from
    rocky to gas dominated planets.
  }
  \label{fig:mass_radius}
\end{figure}

The diagram clearly illustrates the transition from compact rocky
planets to gas-dominated envelopes across the Fulton gap. The PHC
selection lies within the lower-radius regime, consistent with
planets that are likely to retain solid surfaces and potentially
stable atmospheres.

To explore the combined effects of size and irradiation,
Figure~\ref{fig:classmap} shows the full planet population in the
radius - insolation plane, colour-coded by planet class.

\begin{figure}[t!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figs/fig04_classification_map.png}
  \caption{
    Planet classification map in radius - insolation space for all
    6,278 confirmed exoplanets. Colours indicate planet classes.
    The cyan band marks the conservative habitable zone, while the
    red band highlights the Fulton radius gap
    ($1.5$ - $2.0\,\Rearth$). The gold dashed line indicates

  }
  \label{fig:classmap}
\end{figure}

This distribution shows that the vast majority of confirmed planets
orbit well interior to the habitable zone, often receiving orders of
magnitude more stellar flux than Earth. This reflects the strong
selection bias of transit surveys toward short orbital periods.

Figure~\ref{fig:kopparapu} shows the distribution of confirmed planets
in the insolation--radius plane, highlighting the location of
potentially habitable candidates (PHCs).

\begin{figure}[t!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figs/fig05_hz_insolation.png}
  \caption{
    Insolation flux ($S_p$) versus planetary radius for all confirmed
    exoplanets. Grey points represent the full population, while red
    markers indicate the 29 potentially habitable candidates (PHCs).
    The green shaded region denotes the conservative habitable zone
    ($0.2 \leq S_p/\Searth \leq 1.8$), and the blue band marks the
    Earth-sized radius range ($0.5$--$1.5\,\Rearth$).
    The vertical dashed line indicates Earth-equivalent insolation
    ($S_p = 1\,\Searth$). The clustering of confirmed planets at high

  }
  \label{fig:kopparapu}

The distribution highlights that the majority of confirmed planets lie
well interior to the habitable zone, receiving significantly higher
stellar flux than Earth. In contrast, the PHCs occupy a narrow region
where both irradiation and size constraints are simultaneously satisfied.

This representation also shows that the habitable zone around low-mass
stars is more densely populated in the current census, primarily due to
shorter orbital periods that enhance both transit detectability and
radial-velocity follow-up. Systems such as TRAPPIST-1 provide clear
examples, where multiple planets lie within the conservative habitable
zone of a cool host star ($\Teff \approx 2{,}566\,\mathrm{K}$).

\subsection{Metallicity and Planet Type}

The dependence of planet properties on host-star metallicity, along with
the distribution of system multiplicity, is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:metallicity}.

\begin{figure}[t!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figs/fig13_metallicity_multiplicity.png}
  \caption{
    \textit{Left:} Stellar metallicity ($\FeH$) distributions for giant
    planets ($R_p \geq 6\,\Rearth$) and terrestrial planets
    ($R_p < 2\,\Rearth$). Giant planets are preferentially found around
    metal-rich stars, while terrestrial planets span a broader range of
    metallicities centred near solar values.
    \textit{Right:} Distribution of planetary system multiplicity $N_p$.
    Single-planet systems dominate, although systems with multiple planets
    are also common.
  }
  \label{fig:metallicity}
\end{figure}

The metallicity distribution clearly reproduces the well-known
planet-metallicity correlation, in which giant planets are more likely
to form around stars with super-solar metallicities
\citep{Santos2004, Fischer2005}. In contrast, smaller planets are found
around stars spanning a wide range of metallicities, suggesting that
their formation is less sensitive to the initial metal content of the
protoplanetary disk.

The multiplicity distribution shows that most systems host a single
confirmed planet, although compact multi-planet systems are not uncommon.
Such systems provide important constraints on planetary system formation
and dynamical evolution.

Sun-like hosts. These trends reflect both underlying planet-formation
physics and observational selection effects.

The strong clustering of planets in a limited region of the sky reflects
the observing strategy of \textit{Kepler}, which continuously monitored a
single field. In contrast, the more uniform spread of recent detections is
driven by \textit{TESS}, which surveys nearly the entire sky. The PHC sample,
dominated by nearby M-dwarf systems, is not confined to a specific region,
highlighting the growing role of targeted radial-velocity surveys in the
solar neighbourhood.



\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{M-Dwarf Dominance and Habitability}

Most PHCs in our sample orbit M-dwarf stars (22 out of 23 with known
spectral types). This is partly expected, as M dwarfs are the most common
stars in the Galaxy and are well suited for planet detection. Their
habitable zones lie close to the star, making transits more likely and
radial-velocity signals easier to measure.

However, habitability around M dwarfs is still uncertain. Young M dwarfs
emit strong high-energy radiation that can erode planetary atmospheres.
Planets in these systems are also likely to be tidally locked, which may
lead to large temperature differences between their day and night sides.
In addition, frequent stellar flares can significantly alter atmospheric
chemistry. While models suggest that thick atmospheres may stabilise
surface conditions, observational confirmation is still lacking.

\subsection{Nearby Targets: Proxima Centauri and TRAPPIST-1}

The nearest PHC, Proxima~Centauri~b ($d = 1.30$ pc), is particularly
important because it offers the best opportunity for future direct
characterisation. Its size and insolation are similar to Earth, but its
host star is highly active, and it is unclear whether the planet has
retained a stable atmosphere.

The TRAPPIST-1 system represents a different but equally important case.
It contains multiple planets in or near the habitable zone within a
compact orbital configuration. Among them, TRAPPIST-1~e is considered
the most Earth-like based on current constraints. The system provides
a unique laboratory for studying planetary climates under similar
stellar conditions.

\subsection{Occurrence Rates and Implications}

The fraction of PHCs identified in this study is small ($\sim 0.5\%$),
but this is strongly affected by observational bias. Transit surveys are
much more sensitive to short-period planets, meaning that habitable-zone
planets around Sun-like stars are underrepresented.

Corrected occurrence-rate estimates suggest that habitable-zone rocky
planets are common, particularly around M dwarfs. When scaled to the
entire Galaxy, this implies that a very large number of potentially
habitable worlds exist, even within a few kiloparsecs of the Sun.

\subsection{Future Prospects}

Current observations are beginning to probe the atmospheres of nearby
rocky planets, especially those around M dwarfs. The next major step
is the detection of atmospheric molecules that could indicate habitability
or biological activity.

Future missions will play a crucial role in this effort. Space-based
telescopes designed for direct imaging will be able to observe Earth-like
planets around Sun-like stars, while ongoing transit surveys will continue
to expand the sample of nearby targets. Together, these approaches will
help move the field from detection to characterisation.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusions}

We have analysed the current population of confirmed exoplanets to
identify potentially habitable candidates using simple but physically
motivated criteria. Out of 6,278 confirmed planets, 29 satisfy both
habitable-zone insolation limits and Earth-like size constraints.

The present sample is dominated by planets orbiting M-dwarf stars,
reflecting both observational bias and the high occurrence rate of
small planets around low-mass stars. Nearby systems such as Proxima
Centauri and TRAPPIST-1 represent key targets for future atmospheric
studies.

Although the observed fraction of habitable candidates is small, this
is largely due to detection limitations. Statistical corrections suggest
that habitable rocky planets are common in the Galaxy.

With upcoming observational facilities, the focus is expected to shift
from detection to detailed characterisation. The coming decades will
be critical for determining whether any of these worlds host conditions
suitable for life.
